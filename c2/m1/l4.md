# Lesson Overview

## Description

This lesson takes you behind the curtain of Large Language Models (LLMs). Most users interact with chatbots like ChatGPT or Claude without ever understanding how these systems actually learned to speak, reason, follow instructions, and answer questions. This lesson explains the complete training pipeline that transforms raw internet text into a helpful, aligned AI assistant.

You will learn about the transformer architecture, the single most important invention in modern AI, and the attention mechanism that allows models to focus on the most relevant words in a sentence. Then you will explore the three major training stages: pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).

This matters because prompt engineering is not magic. Every prompt you write is interpreted by a model whose behavior was shaped by these training stages. When you understand why a model sometimes answers confidently and incorrectly, or why it follows instructions surprisingly well, you become a far more effective engineer.

This lesson connects directly to the previous lesson, "Introduction to Large Language Models," where you learned that LLMs generate text one token at a time. It also prepares you for the next lesson, "The AI Landscape," where you will see how these trained models are deployed across industry.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain why models like ChatGPT behave so differently from raw pre-trained language models, and you will understand the engineering decisions behind building an AI assistant.

# Why This Topic Matters

Imagine using a calculator every day without knowing what happens when you press a button. You might trust every output, even when the result is wrong. Using an LLM without understanding how it was trained is similar. You may not know why it repeats itself, why it invents facts, or why a small change in your prompt changes the entire answer.

The training pipeline is the answer to all of these questions. The reason GPT-4 can write poetry, solve math problems, and explain quantum physics is not because someone manually taught it these skills. It learned from massive amounts of text during pre-training, then learned to follow instructions during fine-tuning, and finally learned human preferences during RLHF.

Engineers who understand this pipeline make better decisions. For example, if you know that fine-tuning teaches a model to follow instructions, you will understand why giving examples in your prompt (few-shot prompting) is a lighter-weight alternative to fine-tuning. If you know that RLHF shapes model values, you will understand why some models refuse certain requests and why that refusal is a feature, not a bug.

In industry, this knowledge is essential. Companies train or fine-tune models for customer support, medical documentation, code generation, and legal analysis. Teams decide between using a commercial API, fine-tuning an open-source model, or building a custom pipeline. Without understanding the training pipeline, these decisions are guesswork.

Finally, this lesson helps you think like an AI engineer, not just an AI user. Pre-training, fine-tuning, and RLHF are the core engineering stages that turn raw computation into a product. Understanding them is your first step toward building your own LLM-powered applications.

# Introduction

Think about the last time you used autocomplete while writing a text message. You typed two words, and your phone suggested the third. It felt smart, but it was also a little limited. Now imagine an autocomplete system that has read nearly the entire internet: books, Wikipedia, software code, scientific papers, news articles, and millions of conversations. Imagine it is so large that it can finish your sentence, answer your questions, write a poem, and even explain why it made a mistake.

That is essentially what a modern LLM is. But here is the question this lesson answers: **how does it get there?**

A newborn baby does not know language. Over years, the baby hears millions of sentences, begins to predict what people will say next, and then learns the social rules of conversation—when to say please, when not to shout, and how to answer a question helpfully. Training an LLM follows a surprisingly similar path, but at an enormous scale and at incredible speed.

This lesson tells the story of that training journey. You will meet the transformer, the neural network architecture that made modern LLMs possible. You will see how a model "reads" trillions of words during pre-training, how it learns to follow instructions during fine-tuning, and how human preferences are baked in through RLHF.

By the end, you will never look at a chatbot the same way again. You will see it as an engineered system with a specific training history, with strengths and weaknesses that directly trace back to those three stages.

Let us begin with a question that often puzzles beginners: what does it actually mean to "train" a model that is just predicting the next word?

---

# Concept Explanation

At the highest level, training an LLM is the process of adjusting the model's internal parameters so that it becomes better at predicting what text comes next. All LLM behavior—writing, reasoning, summarizing, answering—emerges from this surprisingly simple goal.

Think of the model as a machine with billions of knobs, called **parameters**. Initially, those knobs are set to random values, and the model generates nonsense. During training, the model is shown millions of text examples. For each example, it makes a prediction, compares its prediction with the actual next word, and then slightly adjusts all its knobs to make the prediction more accurate. This is repeated billions of times.

But raw next-word prediction is not enough. If you trained a model only to predict the next word, you would get a fantastic autocomplete but a poor assistant. It might continue your sentence well, but it would not answer "What is the capital of France?" in a helpful, direct way. It might respond, "What is the capital of France is a common trivia question that many people ask because geography is interesting and France is..."

That is why modern LLMs go through multiple training stages. The stages are:

1. **Pre-training:** Learn language patterns from massive text.
2. **Supervised fine-tuning:** Learn to follow instructions using human-written examples.
3. **RLHF:** Learn human preferences, helpfulness, and safety through feedback.

Together, these stages turn a raw text prediction engine into an AI assistant. The rest of this lesson explores each stage in depth, starting with the architecture that makes them all possible: the transformer.

### Attention Mechanism

```
Query: "which word should I look at?"
Key:   "a label that tells me what each word contains"
Value: "the actual meaning of each word"

For each token, the model:
1. Creates a Query (Q)  → What am I looking for?
2. Creates a Key (K)    → What do I contain?
3. Creates a Value (V)  → What information do I carry?

Then it computes an attention score:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d ) V

High score  = "I should pay attention to that word."
Low score   = "That word is not important for my prediction."
```

# Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Parameter | A number inside the model that is adjusted during training. Modern LLMs have billions of parameters. |
| Transformer | A neural network architecture based on self-attention that enables parallel processing of text. |
| Attention | A mechanism that computes which tokens in the input are most relevant to each other when making predictions. |
| Self-Attention | Attention applied within a single sequence, allowing every token to "look at" every other token. |
| Token | The basic unit of text processed by the model; tokens are often sub-words rather than whole words. |
| Pre-training | The first training stage, in which a model learns general language patterns by predicting the next token on massive text corpora. |
| Self-Supervised Learning | A training method where the data provides its own labels—in this case, the next word is the label. |
| Base Model | A model that has only been pre-trained. It predicts text well but is not instruction-following. |
| Fine-tuning | Continued training of a pre-trained model on a narrower dataset to improve a specific behavior. |
| Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) | Fine-tuning using human-written instruction–response pairs so the model learns to follow instructions. |
| RLHF | Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback—training a model using a reward signal derived from human preferences. |
| Reward Model | A separate model trained to predict how much humans would prefer a given model output. |
| Policy | The LLM being trained to generate text; in RLHF, it is the "policy" being optimized. |
| PPO | Proximal Policy Optimization, a reinforcement learning algorithm commonly used in RLHF. |
| Alignment | The process of making model behavior consistent with human values, intentions, and safety requirements. |
| Catastrophic Forgetting | When a model loses previously learned abilities after training on a new, narrow dataset. |
| LoRA | Low-Rank Adaptation, an efficient fine-tuning method that trains a small number of additional parameters rather than all of them. |
| In-Context Learning | Using examples embedded in the prompt to guide a model, without updating its parameters. |

## Concept 1: Transformer Architecture & Attention

### What Is It?

The transformer is a neural network architecture introduced in the 2017 research paper "Attention Is All You Need" by Vaswani et al. It is the architecture behind virtually every modern LLM, including GPT, Claude, and Llama.

Unlike older models that processed text word-by-word in sequence, the transformer processes all tokens in a sentence simultaneously. It achieves this through a mechanism called **self-attention**, which allows the model to weigh the importance of every token relative to every other token.

Imagine you are reading the sentence: "The animal didn't cross the street because **it** was too tired." To understand what "it" refers to, you must look back at "animal." The transformer does exactly this with attention. For each word, it looks at all other words and decides which ones matter most.

### Why Does It Exist?

Before transformers, the dominant architectures for language tasks were recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs). These models processed text in order, maintaining a "memory" as they moved through the sentence. This had a critical weakness: the memory became less reliable as sentences grew longer. Long-range relationships were hard to learn, and the sequential nature of processing meant training was slow because each step depended on the previous step.

Transformers solved both problems. Attention lets the model directly connect any two words in a sentence, no matter how far apart. And because all tokens are processed in parallel, transformers can be trained much faster on modern hardware. This speed enabled scaling to billions of parameters and trillions of training tokens.

### How Does It Work?

At a high level, the transformer does the following:

1. **Embedding:** Each input token is converted into a vector of numbers that represents its meaning.
2. **Positional Encoding:** Because the model processes tokens in parallel, it needs an explicit way to know word order. Positional encodings add position information to each token vector.
3. **Self-Attention:** The model creates three vectors for each token: a Query (Q), a Key (K), and a Value (V). The Query asks, "What am I looking for?" The Key answers, "What do I contain?" The Value carries the actual content. The model computes how well each query matches each key, producing an attention score. The scores are normalized with a softmax function and then used to blend the Values. The result is a new representation for each token that incorporates relevant context from the whole sentence.
4. **Feed-Forward Layers:** After attention, each token is passed through a simple feed-forward neural network that further transforms the information.
5. **Stacking:** Steps 3 and 4 are repeated in many layers. Each layer builds on the previous one, allowing the model to learn increasingly abstract patterns.

The attention formula is commonly written as:

```
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d ) V
```

Here, Q, K, and V are matrices of queries, keys, and values. The √d term is a scaling factor that keeps the dot products from becoming too large.

A key enhancement is **multi-head attention**. Rather than computing attention once, the model computes it several times in parallel, each time with a slightly different projection of Q, K, and V. This is like having multiple different "viewpoints" on the sentence. One head might focus on syntax while another focuses on meaning.

### Where Is It Used?

Transformers are used everywhere in modern AI:

- **Decoder-only models** (GPT, Llama, Mistral) are transformers that generate text token-by-token.
- **Encoder-only models** (BERT) are transformers specialized for understanding rather than generation.
- **Encoder-decoder models** (T5, BART) are transformers that first encode the input then decode an output, useful for translation and summarization.
- Beyond text, transformers power image models (Vision Transformer, DALL-E), audio models (Whisper), and multimodal models (GPT-4V, Claude 3).

### Advantages

- **Parallelization:** Training is vastly faster than with RNNs because tokens are processed simultaneously.
- **Long-range dependencies:** Attention can directly relate distant words, making it excellent for language understanding.
- **Scalability:** The architecture scales predictably with model size and data, enabling the continued growth of LLMs.
- **Flexibility:** The same architecture works for text, images, audio, and more.

### Limitations

- **Quadratic cost:** Attention computes a score for every pair of tokens. For a sequence of length n, this takes O(n²) time and memory. Very long documents become expensive.
- **Static context window:** The model can only attend to a fixed maximum number of tokens. Anything beyond it is simply invisible.
- **No true memory:** Once training is finished, the model cannot recall new conversations unless the conversation is included in the prompt.
- **Interpretability:** Despite attention's transparency at the mechanism level, it is extremely difficult to explain exactly why a model makes a particular prediction.

### Alternatives

- **RNNs and LSTMs:** Older sequential architectures. They are more parameter-efficient for short sequences but struggle with long-range relationships and training speed.
- **State-space models (e.g., Mamba):** More recent architectures that attempt to combine the parallel training advantages of transformers with more efficient and longer context handling.
- **Hybrids:** Some models combine attention with other mechanisms to reduce cost or extend context.

**Analogy:** Think of attention as a spotlight in a dark theater. When the model reads a word, it shines the spotlight on the other words that matter most. The brighter the spotlight, the more attention that word receives. A transformer's multi-head attention is like having several spotlights, each focused on a different aspect of the scene.

## Concept 2: Pre-training

### What Is It?

Pre-training is the first and most expensive stage of LLM development. During pre-training, the model is exposed to a massive amount of text—often trillions of words—and its only task is to predict the next token.

This is called **self-supervised learning** because the data provides its own labels. If the model reads the sentence "The cat sat on the ___", the next word "mat" is the label. No human needs to annotate the data. This is what makes pre-training at internet scale possible: there is far more text in the world than there are human-labeled examples.

### Why Does It Exist?

Language is deeply complex. To answer questions, write essays, or summarize documents, a model must first understand grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and a vast amount of world knowledge. You cannot reasonably hand-label enough examples to teach all of this. Pre-training leverages freely available text to give the model a broad foundation of language ability.

Imagine a medical student who, before studying specific diseases, spends four years reading every textbook in the library. That student learns hundreds of thousands of background facts: how the body works, what common terms mean, how diseases are classified. Later, those background facts make advanced training far more effective. Pre-training is the LLM equivalent of reading the entire library.

### How Does It Work?

1. **Collect data:** The model is trained on books, Wikipedia, web pages, academic papers, code repositories, and other text. This corpus may be trillions of tokens.
2. **Tokenize:** The text is split into tokens. Tokens are usually sub-words, so common words are single tokens while rare words may be split into multiple tokens.
3. **Predict the next token:** For every position in a sentence, the model tries to predict the distribution over the next token. For example, after "The capital of Italy is ___", the correct next token is "Rome".
4. **Compute the loss:** The model's predictions are compared with the actual next tokens using cross-entropy loss. If the model assigns low probability to the correct token, the loss is high.
5. **Backpropagate and update:** Using gradient descent, the model updates its billions of parameters to make the correct next token more likely in the future.
6. **Repeat:** This is done billions of times over many epochs, for weeks or months, on thousands of GPUs.

The mathematical objective is to maximize the probability of the entire training corpus:

```
P(w₁, w₂, ..., w_T) = ∏ P(w_t | w₁, w₂, ..., w_{t-1})
```

Each token is predicted using all of the preceding tokens. This is known as **causal language modeling**.

### Where Is It Used?

Pre-training is used by all major model providers:

- OpenAI pre-trained the GPT series on internet text and books.
- Anthropic pre-trained the Claude series on a similarly massive corpus.
- Meta pre-trained the Llama series and released the base weights to the research community.
- Mistral, Alibaba's Qwen, and many others pre-train their own models.

Once pre-training is complete, the result is called a **base model**. Base models are rarely useful as products. They can continue text fluently, but they do not answer questions directly, follow instructions, or maintain a conversation.

### Advantages

- **Makes use of abundant data:** No human labels are needed, so scale is limited only by compute and data availability.
- **Builds broad knowledge:** The model learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, common sense, and even the ability to perform tasks like translation and summarization simply from text prediction.
- **Creates a reusable foundation:** A single pre-trained base model can be fine-tuned for countless downstream tasks.

### Limitations

- **Extremely expensive:** Pre-training a frontier model can cost tens of millions of dollars in compute.
- **Learn statistical patterns, not verified truth:** The model learns what text tends to follow what text, not what is factually correct. This is the root of hallucination.
- **Reflects data bias:** The internet is filled with bias, misinformation, and harmful content. The model absorbs it.
- **Resource intensive:** Only large companies and research labs can typically afford pre-training from scratch.

### Alternatives

- **Training from scratch:** An alternative, but only appropriate for highly specialized domains with unusual vocabulary and a large budget.
- **Continued pre-training:** A lighter-weight option. Instead of starting from random weights, you start from an existing model and continue pre-training it on additional domain text.
- **Distillation:** Training a smaller model to imitate a larger model, reducing deployment cost.

**Analogy:** Pre-training is like learning a language by reading millions of books. You become fluent in the structure and rhythm of the language, and you pick up endless facts. But you have not yet been trained to answer a stranger's questions politely. That comes next.

## Concept 3: Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)

### What Is It?

Supervised fine-tuning, often abbreviated as SFT, is the second training stage. After pre-training, the model is trained again on a far smaller but high-quality dataset of instruction–response pairs. Each example looks like this:

```
Instruction: "Explain the water cycle in simple terms."
Response: "The water cycle is the continuous movement..."
```

The model learns to generate the response given the instruction. This is called supervised learning because every example has a human-written label—the correct response.

### Why Does It Exist?

A base model is a text completion engine, not an assistant. It does not know that when a human writes "What is the capital of France?", the expected output is a short, direct answer. Instead, it will continue the text in whatever style is statistically likely.

SFT bridges this gap. By training on thousands of high-quality instruction–response pairs, the model learns the *format* and *behavior* of an assistant. It learns when to answer directly, when to use bullet points, when to ask clarifying questions, and how to phrase helpful responses.

This adaptation is the core reason ChatGPT felt revolutionary. Large language models existed before ChatGPT, but they were not trained to follow instructions. The instruction-tuning stage made them usable by ordinary people.

### How Does It Work?

1. **Prepare a dataset:** Curate thousands (or millions) of human-written instruction–response pairs. These may be generated by contractors, sourced from existing support conversations, or written by domain experts.
2. **Choose data quality over quantity:** A few thousand exceptional examples often beat hundreds of thousands of mediocre ones. The goal is to teach the model a style and behavior, not new facts.
3. **Continue training:** Take the pre-trained base model and continue optimizing it on the instruction dataset. The objective is the same cross-entropy loss used in pre-training, but now the correct next tokens are guided toward the desired human response.
4. **Use a lower learning rate:** Because the model is already well-trained, the updates are smaller. This prevents "forgetting" the broad knowledge from pre-training.
5. **Evaluate:** After SFT, the model should be evaluated on hold-out examples and real user prompts to verify that it actually follows instructions well.

### Where Is It Used?

- **Chat models:** GPT-3.5 and GPT-4's instruction-following capabilities come from supervised fine-tuning on instruction datasets.
- **Domain-specific assistants:** A legal research assistant might be fine-tuned on curated Q&A pairs covering contracts, case law, and legal writing style.
- **Code models:** Codex and others were fine-tuned on code and natural-language-to-code pairs.
- **Enterprise deployments:** Many companies fine-tune open-source models like Llama or Mistral on their own internal Q&A data.

### Advantages

- **Behavioral control:** SFT teaches the model how to respond in a specific format and style.
- **Efficiency:** Requires far less data and compute than pre-training.
- **Accessibility:** With techniques like LoRA, fine-tuning can be done on a single consumer GPU.
- **Bridges foundation to product:** SFT is what makes a raw text predictor a usable assistant.

### Limitations

- **Catastrophic forgetting:** If the fine-tuning dataset is too narrow, the model may forget general knowledge from pre-training.
- **Data quality bottleneck:** The quality of the model's instruction-following is directly limited by the quality of the human-written examples.
- **Only captures surface behavior:** SFT teaches the model how to respond, but it does not deeply teach values, safety, or preferences beyond what is in the examples.
- **Still needs alignment:** An SFT model may give harmful answers if the instruction dataset did not explicitly forbid them.

### Alternatives

- **In-context learning (prompting):** Instead of updating parameters, you show the model a few examples in the prompt. This is what you will do extensively in Module 3. It is cheaper and faster but does not permanently change the model.
- **Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, QLoRA):** Instead of updating all billions of parameters, you train a small number of additional parameters. This drastically reduces memory and cost.
- **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation):** For domain knowledge, retrieving relevant documents and placing them in the prompt is often more effective than fine-tuning on domain data.

**Analogy:** If pre-training is like reading the entire library, SFT is like taking a class on how to answer questions at a help desk. You learn the tone, the format, and the expected behavior. You have not gained a ton of new knowledge, but you now know *how* to use what you already know in conversation.

## Concept 4: RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

### What Is It?

RLHF is the third and final major training stage. It uses human preferences to train a **reward model**, which is then used to further optimize the LLM through reinforcement learning.

The core idea is simple: instead of hoping the model follows instructions well because we showed it examples, we explicitly teach it *what humans prefer*. Human raters compare two or more model outputs and say which one is better. The reward model learns to predict these preferences. Then the LLM is trained to produce outputs that the reward model scores highly.

### Why Does It Exist?

SFT teaches the model the *format* of a helpful assistant, but it does not teach the model *values*. Consider two possible responses to the request "Give me advice on starting a small business." Both may be grammatically correct and formatted well. But one may be more helpful, more honest about risks, and more encouraging. The other may be generic, misleading, or even dangerous.

Humans have complex preferences. Rather than trying to hand-write examples for every possible scenario, RLHF captures these preferences from human judgments. It makes the model not just instruction-following, but aligned with what people actually want: helpful, honest, and safe responses.

### How Does It Work?

RLHF typically proceeds in three sub-steps:

1. **Collect human comparisons.** The SFT model generates several responses to a prompt. Human raters rank them from best to worst. For example:
   - Prompt: "Explain how a car engine works."
   - Response A: "An engine turns fuel into motion. First, fuel mixes with air..."
   - Response B: "cAr go vroom vroom brum brum"
   - Human preference: A over B, obviously.
   Many comparisons are collected, often hundreds of thousands.

2. **Train a reward model.** A separate model is trained to predict the human preference. Given a prompt and a response, it outputs a score. Higher score means "humans would probably prefer this." The reward model learns to encode human values into a single number.

3. **Optimize the policy with reinforcement learning.** The LLM (called the "policy") is now trained to maximize the reward model's score. It generates responses, the reward model scores them, and the policy is updated to produce higher-scoring responses in the future. The most common algorithm is PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization).

A key detail is that the model is not allowed to drift too far from the SFT model. A **KL penalty** is added to the objective, which prevents the model from producing bizarre text that would fool the reward model. The full objective is roughly:

```
maximize  Reward(response)  −  β × KL(policy, reference_policy)
```

The KL term keeps the new model close to the SFT model while still improving according to the reward function.

### Where Is It Used?

- **ChatGPT and GPT-4:** OpenAI used RLHF to make ChatGPT helpful, honest, and safer.
- **Claude:** Anthropic used RLHF as part of its "Constitutional AI" approach, combining human feedback with explicit principles.
- **Llama 2 / Llama 3 Chat:** Meta released versions aligned with RLHF.
- **OpenAssistant and other open-source projects:** Use human preferences to align open models.

### Advantages

- **Captures complex human preferences:** Humans can judge helpfulness and safety, which are difficult to specify as rules.
- **Improves helpfulness and safety:** RLHF dramatically reduces toxic, misleading, or unhelpful responses.
- **Generalizes beyond training examples:** The reward model can score novel prompts and responses, guiding the policy toward better behavior broadly.
- **Enables fine-grained control:** You can steer the model toward particular qualities—politeness, conciseness, or creativity—by choosing which responses humans rank higher.

### Limitations

- **Reward hacking:** The model may find a way to produce high-reward text that humans would not actually like. This is why the KL penalty is essential.
- **Goodhart's Law:** "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." If the reward model is imperfect, the policy may exploit its blind spots.
- **Very expensive:** Collecting human preferences at scale is costly, and training with PPO is computationally intensive.
- **Can make models sycophantic:** RLHF can train models to agree with users too often, because agreement often receives high human preference.
- **Value alignment is subjective:** Different humans, cultures, and contexts have different preferences. Whose values are being encoded?

### Alternatives

- **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization):** A more recent method that optimizes the model directly on human preferences without a separate reward model and PPO loop. It is simpler and often cheaper.
- **RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback):** Instead of human raters, another strong AI model provides the preferences. This is part of Anthropic's Constitutional AI.
- **Constitutional AI:** The model critiques and revises its own outputs according to a constitution of explicit principles, reducing the need for extensive human labeling.
- **Red-teaming and safety filters:** Post-training filters and moderation systems can catch harmful outputs, but they do not change the model's values in the way RLHF does.

**Analogy:** Imagine a student (the LLM) learns how to answer questions in class (SFT). The teacher (the reward model) then observes the student's answers and gives feedback: "Good, but you should be more concise," or "That is technically true, but it would scare the patient—say it more gently." RLHF is the process of the student adjusting their behavior based on that ongoing feedback. In an ideal case, the student ultimately internalizes the teacher's standards and no longer needs constant feedback.

## Concept 5: Base Model vs Instruction-Tuned Model

### What Is It?

The distinction between base models and instruction-tuned models is one of the most practical concepts in this lesson. A **base model** (also called a foundation model) has only been pre-trained. An **instruction-tuned model** has gone through SFT and often RLHF.

This distinction determines how you prompt the model, what behaviors you expect, and what tasks you can reasonably ask it to perform.

### Why Does It Exist?

Base models and instruction-tuned models serve different purposes. Base models are the raw material: they contain broad knowledge but are hard to control. Instruction-tuned models are the finished product: they behave like assistants, follow instructions, and are safe to deploy to users.

Two models can share the exact same underlying knowledge but behave completely differently because of the training stages. Data scientists frequently observe that a base model and its instruction-tuned sibling respond differently to the same prompt.

### How Does It Work?

- **Base model behavior:** Given the prompt "Explain the water cycle," a base model might continue: "Explain the water cycle is a common elementary school assignment that asks students to describe how water moves through the environment. In this essay, we will discuss evaporation and condensation..."
- **Instruction-tuned behavior:** Given the same prompt, the instruction-tuned model answers directly: "The water cycle is the process by which water moves between the earth's surface and the atmosphere through evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection."

The instruction-tuned model has learned a specific *pattern*: when a user writes an imperative instruction, the correct continuation is the requested answer, not a continuation of the sentence.

From the developer's perspective, this difference is enormous. With an instruction-tuned model, a simple prompt like "Translate this to French: I love you" works. With a base model, you would need to craft the text very carefully, often using few-shot examples, to coax the model into performing tasks.

### Where Is It Used?

- **Base models:** Used by researchers and engineers who want to fine-tune for a custom purpose. Hugging Face hosts thousands of base models.
- **Instruction-tuned models:** Used in products, chatbots, coding assistants, and APIs. When you call `gpt-3.5-turbo` or `claude-3`, you are using an instruction-tuned model.
- **Open-source ecosystem:** Many open-source releases include both versions, e.g., `meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-hf` (base) and `meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-chat-hf` (instruction-tuned).

### Advantages

- **Base model advantages:** Retains the pure "text continuation" style, which can be useful for fine-tuning on a specific domain without inheriting the chat model's conversational formatting. It is also often the only option if you want maximum control over the final behavior.
- **Instruction-tuned model advantages:** Ready to use, follows natural-language instructions, handles dialogue, refuses harmful requests, and produces more helpful and concise output.

### Limitations

- **Base model limitations:** Hard to prompt, poor at following instructions, may produce harmful text, and is generally not product-ready.
- **Instruction-tuned model limitations:** May be too "chatty," may refuse benign requests due to over-alignment, can be sycophantic, and may have lost some raw text-completion ability.

### Alternatives

- **In-context learning:** You can use a base model with a few carefully chosen examples in the prompt to approximate instruction-following. This is less reliable but avoids the cost of fine-tuning.
- **Small, task-specific fine-tunes:** For narrow tasks, you can fine-tune a base model on only a few hundred examples rather than using a general instruction-tuned model.
- **API-level system prompts:** Many commercial APIs already include instruction-tuned models, so you rarely need to touch base models unless you are doing custom training.

**Analogy:** A base model is like a brilliant but socially awkward scholar who gives rambling mini-lectures when asked a simple question. An instruction-tuned model is that same scholar after training as a customer service representative: still brilliant, but now focused, direct, and aware of the user's needs.

## Example 1: Daily Life — The Chef's Journey

**Problem:** A new restaurant wants to hire a chef. The candidate has a culinary degree but has never worked in a professional kitchen that serves Italian food. The restaurant needs the chef to cook their signature dishes consistently and follow customer feedback.

**Thought Process:**

- First, what does the chef already know? A culinary degree means broad knowledge of ingredients, techniques, and recipes.
- Second, what is missing? The chef does not know this restaurant's menu, plating style, or customer preferences.
- Third, how will the chef learn the missing pieces? Through training in the kitchen, then through feedback when customers complain or compliment dishes.

This maps directly to the LLM training pipeline: the culinary degree is pre-training, the on-the-job training is supervised fine-tuning, and the customer feedback is RLHF.

**Solution:**

- **Pre-training stage:** The chef graduated from culinary school after years of study across all cuisines. This is broad knowledge, not specific to the restaurant.
- **Supervised fine-tuning stage:** During the first month, the chef observes the head chef, learns the restaurant's recipes, and practices preparing dishes while following written instructions. This is like training the model on instruction–response pairs.
- **RLHF stage:** After opening, customers rate dishes. The sous chef reports which plates come back empty and which get complaints. The head chef adjusts the recipe accordingly. The chef learns which choices people like.

**Rationale:**

The solution works because it mirrors how humans acquire expertise. Broad foundational learning comes first, specialized learning comes second, and refinement based on feedback comes last. Any step skipped would leave the chef unable to perform. Skipping culinary school means no fundamentals. Skipping kitchen training means no specialty. Ignoring customer feedback means the restaurant may fail despite a skilled chef.

**Alternative Solutions:**

- The restaurant could hire a chef already specialized in Italian food. This is analogous to starting with a pre-trained or fine-tuned model rather than training from scratch. It is faster but more expensive.
- The restaurant could rely on a recipe book only, hoping every customer is satisfied. This is analogous to relying only on prompts without fine-tuning or alignment. It is cheap but fragile.
- The restaurant could collect customer feedback manually and update each recipe by hand. This is analogous to manual prompt engineering rather than RLHF.

**Engineering Discussion:**

The analogy highlights why the LLM pipeline is ordered. Pre-training is the most expensive stage and conveys the broadest knowledge. Fine-tuning is comparatively cheap and narrow. RLHF is about taste and values, which are impossible to specify in a rulebook. In engineering terms, each stage has a different cost and a different goal. The restaurant example also shows a key danger: if the chef only listens to the loudest customer complaints (the reward model is imperfect), the menu might become strange and inconsistent—reward hacking in the culinary world.

## Example 2: Platform Example — Observing a Base Model on Your Laptop

**Problem:** You have just learned about pre-training and base models. You want to see with your own eyes what a base model does before any instruction tuning. Which model can you run locally, and how do you observe its next-token prediction behavior?

**Thought Process:**

- You need a small model that can run on a laptop. GPT-2 is an older, small (124M parameter) transformer that is safe and widely available.
- You need a Python library. The Hugging Face `transformers` library provides easy access.
- You need a clear way to observe the model's behavior. A useful experiment is to ask the base model a question and see whether it answers like an assistant or simply continues the text.

**Solution:**

```python
# Import the pipeline module from Hugging Face transformers
from transformers import pipeline

# Load a small base model (pre-trained only, no instruction tuning)
generator = pipeline("text-generation", model="distilgpt2")

# Observe how a base model responds to a direct question
prompt = "What is the capital of France?"
outputs = generator(prompt, max_new_tokens=30, num_return_sequences=2)

for i, output in enumerate(outputs):
    print(f"--- Generation {i+1} ---")
    print(output["generated_text"])
    print()
```

**Expected output (illustrative):**

```
--- Generation 1 ---
What is the capital of France? The answer to the question is that France has a capital city and this city is Paris...

--- Generation 2 ---
What is the capital of France? I am not sure if you are referring to the country of France or to the capital of France...
```

The model does not give the crisp answer "Paris." It continues the text as though the question were part of an essay or a conversation, because that is what its training data looked like.

**Rationale:**

This experiment works because `distilgpt2` is a base model. It was trained only to predict the next token on internet text, not to answer questions. Its output is the statistically most likely continuation of "What is the capital of France?," which in many web pages is a discussion of the question itself. The same prompt given to an instruction-tuned model would produce a direct answer, demonstrating the difference made by fine-tuning.

**Alternative Solutions:**

- Use the full GPT-2 model instead of distilgpt2 for slightly better quality.
- Use a Hugging Face chat model, such as `HuggingFaceH4/zephyr-7b-beta`, to compare behavior, though it is larger and slower on a laptop.
- Use an API model like GPT-4o or Claude with a simple prompt to observe instruction-following, at the cost of a network call.

**Engineering Discussion:**

This example is a perfect demonstration of the difference between base and instruction-tuned models. It also illustrates why prompt engineering techniques differ. Base models often require carefully structured prompts and few-shot examples, while instruction-tuned models respond to plain language. For a platform-level learner, running small base models on a laptop is a low-cost way to build intuition about the training pipeline without spending API credits.

## Example 3: Applied System — Fine-tuning a Small Model on Instruction Data

**Problem:** You have a small base model (distilgpt2) and a handful of instruction-response examples. You want to fine-tune it with supervised training so it gives direct answers to basic questions. You also want to observe that the training loss decreases, proving the model is learning.

**Thought Process:**

- Use the Hugging Face `Trainer` API, which abstracts away most training boilerplate.
- Prepare a tiny dataset of instruction-style examples, formatted as text strings.
- Tokenize the dataset.
- Use a small number of training steps so the example runs quickly on a CPU.
- After training, generate text from the fine-tuned model and compare with the base model.

**Solution:**

```python
from transformers import (
    AutoTokenizer,
    AutoModelForCausalLM,
    Trainer,
    TrainingArguments,
)
from datasets import Dataset

# 1. Create a small instruction-style dataset
examples = {
    "text": [
        "Question: What is the capital of France?\nAnswer: Paris.",
        "Question: What is 2+2?\nAnswer: 4.",
        "Question: What color is the sky?\nAnswer: Blue.",
        "Question: Who wrote Hamlet?\nAnswer: William Shakespeare.",
        "Question: What is water made of?\nAnswer: Hydrogen and oxygen.",
    ]
}
dataset = Dataset.from_dict(examples)

# 2. Load the base model and tokenizer
model_name = "distilgpt2"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name)

# Set a padding token so all examples have the same length
if tokenizer.pad_token is None:
    tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token

# 3. Tokenize the dataset
def tokenize_function(examples):
    return tokenizer(
        examples["text"],
        truncation=True,
        max_length=64,
        padding="max_length",
    )

tokenized_dataset = dataset.map(tokenize_function)

# 4. Configure lightweight training arguments
training_args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="./tiny-finetuned",
    max_steps=20,
    per_device_train_batch_size=2,
    logging_steps=5,
    save_strategy="no",
)

# 5. Train the model
trainer = Trainer(
    model=model,
    args=training_args,
    train_dataset=tokenized_dataset,
)
trainer.train()

# 6. Generate a response with the fine-tuned model
inputs = tokenizer("Question: What is the capital of France?\nAnswer:", return_tensors="pt")
outputs = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=10)
print(tokenizer.decode(outputs[0], skip_special_tokens=True))
```

**Expected output:**

```
Question: What is the capital of France?
Answer: Paris.
```

Before fine-tuning, the model would have continued in a rambling way. After fine-tuning on a few examples, it learns the direct answer pattern.

**Rationale:**

The base model has all the knowledge it needs ("Paris" is a very likely next token after "capital of France"), but it does not know that after the phrase "Answer:" it should produce a direct answer. The fine-tuning examples teach it this output pattern. Since the loss function is cross-entropy, the model adjusts its parameters to increase the probability of "Paris" and other target answers in similar contexts.

**Alternative Solutions:**

- Use LoRA instead of full fine-tuning to reduce memory and training time; this trains only a small adapter.
- Use few-shot prompting instead of fine-tuning: include the same five examples in the prompt, avoiding training entirely.
- Use a much larger pre-trained model that already follows instructions, avoiding fine-tuning altogether.

**Engineering Discussion:**

This example demonstrates the principle behind industrial instruction tuning, though at a toy scale. In production, datasets contain hundreds of thousands of examples, training runs for hours or days, and models are hundreds of billions of parameters. The toy example also reveals a limitation: the model learns the *pattern* of direct answers but may not learn many new *facts*. It already knew that Paris is the capital of France. Fine-tuning mainly teaches behavior, not knowledge. This is why companies often pair fine-tuning with RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) for factual accuracy.

## Example 4: Industrial Physical AI Deployment — Aligning a Customer-Support LLM

**Problem:** An industrial automation company wants to deploy an LLM-powered customer-support assistant for its robotics arm products. The assistant must answer technical questions, follow company policy (never diagnose over-voltage damage without a technician), stay within the product's guaranteed specifications, and refuse requests for unsafe modifications. The company has a large corpus of support tickets and technical documentation, but also a strict budget. How should they train or select the model?

**Thought Process:**

- The company needs an assistant that follows instructions and reflects their safety policy. A raw base model is unacceptable.
- They could use a commercial API with a system prompt, but they want control over policy and data privacy.
- They decide to start from an open-source instruction-tuned model, then apply additional supervised fine-tuning on their support transcripts to teach the exact product vocabulary and style.
- They recognize that SFT alone will not make the model reliably refuse unsafe requests. They need alignment. Because they lack the resources for a full RLHF pipeline, they evaluate two options: full RLHF (with a reward model trained on human preference data) or a simpler alignment method like DPO. They choose DPO for cost efficiency.
- Finally, they deploy a guardrail layer that reviews all outputs against a policy checklist, since no training pipeline is perfect.

**Solution:**

1. **Select a base:** Use `Llama-3-8B-Instruct` (already instruction-tuned and somewhat aligned).
2. **Curate SFT data:** Extract 50,000 high-quality support ticket pairs, each cleaned and verified by a technical writer. Include both normal answers and policy-sensitive refusals.
3. **Supervised fine-tune:** Train the model on these pairs using QLoRA to reduce cost.
4. **Collect preference data:** Have support engineers rank model outputs on 10,000 prompts, focusing on safety and helpfulness.
5. **Align with DPO:** Use the preference data to further train the model with Direct Preference Optimization, avoiding the complexity of PPO.
6. **Evaluate before deployment:** Run red-teaming tests, policy-violation checks, and factual-accuracy evaluations on a hold-out set.
7. **Monitor in production:** Log every response, flag low-confidence answers, and route them to human technicians.

**Rationale:**

This pipeline works because each stage has a purpose. Starting from an instruction-tuned model gives the company a strong base for free. SFT on their own data teaches product-specific knowledge and tone. DPO aligns the model with their safety preferences without the high cost of full RLHF. Continuous evaluation and monitoring protect against the residual imperfections of any training pipeline.

**Alternative Solutions:**

- Use a commercial API (GPT-4o, Claude) with a detailed system prompt and RAG over their documentation. This is faster to deploy, but data privacy, cost per token, and policy control become concerns.
- Full RLHF with PPO: would provide more robust alignment but requires significant ML engineering resources and a large human preference dataset.
- No alignment, only SFT: would be cheaper but would likely produce unsafe answers on edge cases, which is unacceptable for an industrial product.

**Engineering Discussion:**

This industrial example illustrates the real engineering trade-offs behind LLM deployment. Alignment is not a one-time step; it requires continuous evaluation, red-teaming, and monitoring. The cost of a pipeline stage must be weighed against the safety requirements of the domain. In robotics and industrial automation, a hallucinated answer about motor voltage limits could cause equipment damage or injury. That is why this company invests in both training and guardrails. The same logic applies to any high-stakes domain: healthcare, finance, aviation, or autonomous systems.

## Code Example 1: Observing Next-Token Probabilities

This code shows how a base model assigns probabilities to candidate next tokens. It makes the "next-token prediction" concept concrete.

```python
# Import libraries
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
import torch

# Load a small base model and its tokenizer
model_name = "distilgpt2"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name)

# The prompt ends before the next token we want to predict
prompt = "The capital of France is"

# Tokenize the input
inputs = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors="pt")

# Get logits (scores) for each possible next token
with torch.no_grad():
    outputs = model(**inputs)
    logits = outputs.logits

# Focus on the last token position
last_token_logits = logits[0, -1, :]

# Convert logits to probabilities
probabilities = torch.softmax(last_token_logits, dim=-1)

# Find the top 5 most likely next tokens
top_k = torch.topk(probabilities, k=5)

# Print the results
print("Prompt:", prompt)
print(f"{'Token ID':<10} {'Token':<15} {'Probability':<12}")
print("-" * 40)
for i in range(5):
    token_id = top_k.indices[i].item()
    token = tokenizer.decode([token_id])
    prob = top_k.values[i].item()
    print(f"{token_id:<10} {token!r:<15} {prob:.4f}")
```

**Expected output (illustrative):**

```
Prompt: The capital of France is
Token ID   Token           Probability
----------------------------------------
2330       ' Paris'        0.1234
465        ' a'            0.0801
610        ' the'          0.0567
2617       ' not'          0.0342
397        ' one'          0.0289
```

The model assigns the highest probability to "Paris" but is still somewhat uncertain. This is exactly the behavior that pre-training produces: statistical language prediction, not guaranteed factual knowledge.

## Code Example 3: A Minimal RLHF-Style Preference Loop (Conceptual)

This code is not a full RLHF implementation, but it simulates the central idea: sample responses, use a reward signal, and keep the response with higher reward. It helps build intuition for RLHF.

```python
# Simulate an LLM responding to a prompt (returns a tuple: response, reward)
def simulate_model_response(prompt):
    # In a real system, a neural LLM generates these.
    responses_and_rewards = [
        ("The capital of France is Paris.", 0.95),
        ("The capital of France is probably Paris, but I'm not sure.", 0.65),
        ("The capital of France is a number of things and it all depends.", 0.25),
    ]
    return responses_and_rewards

# Simulate the policy improvement step
def rlhf_step(prompt, num_samples=3):
    sampled = [simulate_model_response(prompt)[0] for _ in range(num_samples)]
    scored = [(r, simulate_model_response(prompt)[1]) for r in sampled]
    best = max(scored, key=lambda x: x[1])
    return best

prompt = "What is the capital of France?"
best_response, best_reward = rlhf_step(prompt)
print(f"Prompt: {prompt}")
print(f"Best response: {best_response}")
print(f"Best reward: {best_reward}")
```

This demonstrates the abstract RLHF flow: generate, score, select, and then (in a real system) update the policy to make high-scoring responses more likely.

# Mathematical Explanation

### Objective: Maximize the Probability of the Training Text

During pre-training, an LLM models the probability of a sequence of tokens. For a sequence of T tokens, w₁ through w_T, the probability is the product of conditional probabilities:

```
P(w₁, w₂, ..., w_T) = ∏_{t=1}^{T} P(w_t | w₁, w₂, ..., w_{t-1})
```

This equation says: "The probability of the whole sentence equals the probability of each word given all the words before it."

### Variable Definitions

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| P | Probability |
| w₁, w₂, ..., w_T | The tokens in a training example |
| T | Total number of tokens in the sequence |
| P(w_t \| w₁, ..., w_{t-1}) | The probability the model assigns to token w_t given all previous tokens |

### Loss: Negative Log-Likelihood

In practice, probabilities are extremely small numbers, so multiplying them is unstable. Instead, we maximize the log-probability, equivalently minimizing the negative log-likelihood:

```
Loss = − (1/T) Σ_{t=1}^{T} log P(w_t | w₁, ..., w_{t-1})
```

This is the cross-entropy loss. A lower loss means the model is better at predicting the correct next token.

### Intuition

Consider the sentence "The capital of Italy is Rome." The model reads "The capital of Italy is" and predicts a probability distribution over all possible next tokens. We want a high probability on "Rome."

Suppose the model assigns probability 0.6 to "Rome." Then:

```
Loss = −log(0.6) ≈ 0.51
```

Now suppose after more training, the model assigns probability 0.95 to "Rome." Then:

```
Loss = −log(0.95) ≈ 0.05
```

The loss dropped, and the model becomes more accurate. During training, the model updates its parameters to reduce this loss on billions of examples.

### Worked Example

At a particular training step, the model is given the prefix "The cat sat on the" and the correct next token is "mat."

The model outputs these probabilities:

| Next token | Model probability |
|------------|-------------------|
| mat | 0.45 |
| floor | 0.20 |
| table | 0.15 |
| rug | 0.05 |
| other | 0.15 |

The loss for this step is:

```
Loss = −log(0.45) ≈ 0.80
```

The optimizer updates the weights to increase the probability of "mat" in this context. After many such updates, the model will produce accurate continuations.

### Practical Interpretation

This objective explains why LLMs are so good at some things and so bad at others. The model learns to predict the most *statistically likely* next word, not the *factually true* next word. If the training corpus contains more "the sky is blue" than "the sky is green," the model will prefer blue, even if a particular scene is green.

The same principle underlies hallucination. When the model encounters a prompt about a rare topic, it predicts the most likely continuation based on its training data. This continuation may be completely false, but it is statistically plausible. Understanding this equation is the first step toward designing prompts and evaluation systems that account for this behavior.

# Design Considerations

When engineers train or deploy LLMs, they face a series of design trade-offs across the entire pipeline.

### Performance

The size of the model and amount of pre-training data directly affect quality. Larger models generally perform better but require more compute. Fine-tuning can improve performance on a specific task at the cost of some general ability.

### Cost

Pre-training a frontier model costs millions of dollars. Fine-tuning is much cheaper but still non-trivial. RLHF adds the cost of human raters and reinforcement learning compute. Engineers must decide whether a cheaper alternative—such as prompting, RAG, or small fine-tuned models—is sufficient.

### Data Quality

The quality of the fine-tuning dataset is the strongest predictor of final behavior. A small, clean, carefully curated dataset often outperforms a large, noisy one. In RLHF, the quality of preference judgments matters just as much. Biased or inconsistent raters will produce a biased reward model.

### Reliability

Models trained with SFT and RLHF are more reliable at following instructions, but no model is perfectly reliable. Evaluation must be continuous. In industrial settings, a guardrail layer that reviews outputs before they reach users is often essential.

### Safety

RLHF reduces harmful behavior, but it does not eliminate it. Red-teaming and adversarial testing are necessary. Safety considerations also include privacy: fine-tuning on customer data may expose that data in unexpected ways.

### Maintainability

Models and datasets change. An LLM-trained assistant may need retraining as products, policies, or language evolve. Maintaining a pipeline for data collection, retraining, evaluation, and deployment is a major engineering responsibility, not a one-time task.

### Scalability

As user demand grows, serving a model efficiently becomes critical. Small, fine-tuned models are cheaper to serve than giant ones. Quantization and distillation reduce cost at the expense of some quality.

### Alignment with Product Goals

Every design decision should be traced back to a product requirement. A medical assistant must prioritize safety. A creative writing assistant may prioritize variety. A code assistant may prioritize correctness. The training pipeline should be tuned accordingly.

# Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Cause | Solution |
|----------|-------|----------|
| Confusing pre-training with fine-tuning | The two stages both involve training on text, so beginners often blur them. | Remember: pre-training teaches language from raw text; fine-tuning teaches behavior from instruction–response pairs. |
| Believing a base model is "broken" | A base model gives rambling output because it was not trained to follow instructions, not because it is malfunctioning. | Use an instruction-tuned model for conversational tasks, or prompt a base model with explicit few-shot examples. |
| Thinking LLMs learned facts by memorizing a database | LLMs learn statistical patterns, not verified facts. | Always treat LLM outputs as probabilistic predictions and verify critical facts. |
| Assuming RLHF makes models perfect | RLHF improves alignment but is imperfect and can introduce sycophancy or reward hacking. | Use evaluation, red-teaming, and guardrails even after RLHF. |
| Fine-tuning on too little or too narrow data | The model may overfit and lose generality (catastrophic forgetting). | Mix general instruction data with domain data, or use parameter-efficient methods like LoRA. |
| Confusing the reward model with the LLM | The reward model scores outputs; the policy (LLM) is what generates the outputs. | Keep the two roles separate in your mental model and in code. |
| Ignoring the cost of the pipeline | Training all stages from scratch is extremely expensive. | Start from an open-source pre-trained or instruction-tuned model. Use prompt engineering before considering fine-tuning. |
| Believing prompt engineering can replace all training | Prompting can adapt a model in-context, but it cannot permanently add knowledge or align values. | Use prompting for quick tasks, fine-tuning for behavioral changes, and RAG for factual grounding. |

# Best Practices

- **Start with the smallest model that works.** Do not pre-train or fine-tune large models when a small model plus a good prompt is sufficient.
- **Never pre-train from scratch unless absolutely necessary.** Use open-source pre-trained models and fine-tune them.
- **Use parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA/QLoRA)** for most custom tasks. It is dramatically cheaper and often nearly as good as full fine-tuning.
- **Curate a high-quality dataset.** A few thousand carefully chosen examples are more valuable than hundreds of thousands of noisy ones.
- **Preserve a validation set.** Always evaluate on examples the model has not seen during training.
- **Use system prompts as a lightweight alignment tool** before investing in RLHF. For many products, a strong system prompt plus moderation is enough.
- **Monitor after deployment.** Collect real user prompts, periodic evaluations, and feedback loops to detect drift or degradation.
- **Track training runs with structured logging.** Record datasets, hyperparameters, losses, and evaluation metrics so results are reproducible.
- **Document the training pipeline.** Future engineers should be able to understand what data shaped the model and why.

# Summary

In this lesson, you explored the complete training pipeline behind modern LLMs.

First, you learned about the **transformer architecture**, the neural network design that enabled modern AI. The key idea is self-attention, which allows the model to weigh the importance of every token relative to every other token. This replaced older sequential architectures and made large-scale parallel training possible.

Second, you learned about **pre-training**, the vast, self-supervised first stage. The model reads trillions of tokens and learns to predict the next token. The result is a base model with broad language ability but poor instruction-following.

Third, you learned about **supervised fine-tuning (SFT)**, where the model is trained on human-written instruction–response pairs. This teaches it to behave like an assistant, but it does not deeply encode values or safety.

Fourth, you learned about **RLHF**, which uses human preferences to train a reward model, and then optimizes the LLM (the policy) to produce outputs that humans prefer. RLHF is what makes models helpful, honest, and safe, though it has its own failure modes such as reward hacking and sycophancy.

Finally, you compared **base models and instruction-tuned models**, and you saw how the same prompt produces totally different behavior depending on the training stage.

These concepts are not just theory. They explain why prompt engineering works, why models hallucinate, why some models refuse certain requests, and why commercial APIs behave the way they do.

# Key Takeaways

- Modern LLMs are created through three stages: pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and RLHF.
- Pre-training teaches language and broad knowledge by predicting the next token from massive text.
- Fine-tuning teaches behavior and instruction-following from human-written examples.
- RLHF teaches values and preferences by optimizing against a reward model trained on human judgments.
- Base models predict text; instruction-tuned models answer questions after being prompted.
- Attention is the core mechanism of the transformer and enables the model to focus on relevant context.
- Every training stage has limitations: pre-training is expensive, fine-tuning can overfit, and RLHF can introduce reward hacking.
- Understanding training helps you make better decisions about prompting, fine-tuning, and model selection.
